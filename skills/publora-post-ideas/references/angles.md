# Angle library

Forty starting points, eight categories, five each. Each one is an *invitation*,
not a template to fill in — the post that comes out should sound like the person,
not like the entry.

**How to read an entry.** The heading is the angle. The paragraph is what makes
it worth posting. **Core** is the single fact that must come from the user; ask
for it before drafting, and never supply it yourself.

Categories, in the order they appear: Story · Behind the scenes · Tip ·
Case study · List · How-to · Opinion · Question.

---

## Story

### 🩹 The lesson from a specific mistake
Something you got wrong that changed how you work since. Small ones work as well
as expensive ones — the lesson only has to be specific enough that someone else
can borrow it without having made the same mistake.
**Core:** the mistake, named plainly, and the thing you now do differently.

### 🚪 The thing you quit
A client, a channel, a format, a habit that everyone said was essential. Quitting
is under-posted because it feels like an admission; it reads as clarity.
**Core:** what you stopped, and what got better once you did.

### 🐌 The result that took far longer than anyone promised
Most advice comes with an implied timeline, and most timelines are wrong. If
something took you eight months that the internet said would take six weeks,
that gap is the post.
**Core:** the promised timeline, the real one, and what filled the difference.

### 🎒 A skill from an old job you still use every week
Careers rarely start where they end up, and the useful parts travel. Something
from a job you no longer mention shows up in your work constantly.
**Core:** the old job, the specific skill, and where it surfaced most recently.

### 🫥 The moment you almost weren't doing this at all
A near-miss: the offer you nearly took, the month you nearly stopped, the
decision that could easily have gone the other way. Nobody's path is as
deliberate as it looks in a bio.
**Core:** the fork, and the small thing that decided it.

## Behind the scenes

### 🚧 The middle of the thing, before it works
Finished work is polished into unrecognisability. The half-built version — with
the placeholder text and the part that keeps breaking — is more useful and far
rarer.
**Core:** what you are in the middle of right now, and what is not working yet.

### 🧾 What it actually cost
Time, money, or both, itemised. People plan against numbers they have never
seen, and almost nobody publishes theirs.
**Core:** the real figures, however unflattering.

### 🗑️ The version you threw away
Before the thing that shipped, there was one that did not. Showing the rejected
version and saying why makes the shipped one legible.
**Core:** what the discarded version looked like, and the reason it lost.

### ⏱️ One hour of your week, in detail
Not the routine, not the ideal day — one real hour, with the interruptions in.
Specificity is the whole effect here; a generalised day is a horoscope.
**Core:** which hour, and what actually happened in it.

### 🧰 The tool you'd be slightly embarrassed to admit you still use
Everyone's stack has one entry that has survived every migration: a spreadsheet,
a notes file, a fifteen-year-old script. That one is more interesting than the
polished stack.
**Core:** the tool, and the thing it does that nothing newer has replaced.

## Tip

### 🎯 One setting almost nobody changes
A default you turned off, a checkbox you found, a config line that changed how a
tool behaves. Small, concrete, immediately actionable.
**Core:** the exact setting and the difference it made.

### ♻️ The shortcut you built for yourself
A snippet, a checklist, a saved search, a template. Things people build for
themselves are usually better than things built to be sold.
**Core:** what it is, and what it replaced.

### 🧪 The small test with a clear result
Not a growth study — one change, one comparison, one outcome. Two subject lines,
two posting times, two thumbnails.
**Core:** what you compared, and the actual result including the size of it.

### 🚫 The advice you would now tell people to ignore
Widely repeated guidance that you followed, and that cost you. This is more
useful than a tip, because it saves time rather than adding a task.
**Core:** the advice, and what following it cost you specifically.

### 📐 Your rule of thumb, and where it breaks
Everyone runs on heuristics they have never written down. Write one down —
then, more importantly, say the case where it fails.
**Core:** the rule, and the exception that proves it has limits.

## Case study

### 📉 The thing that didn't work, with numbers
A campaign, a launch, a channel that went nowhere. Failures with figures
attached are read far more carefully than successes without them.
**Core:** what you tried, what you expected, and what you got.

### 🔍 A before and after, with the middle shown
Anyone can post two screenshots. The value is in the steps between them,
including the ones that turned out to be detours.
**Core:** the starting state, the end state, and at least one wrong turn.

### 🧭 A decision you reversed, and what the reversal cost
Changing course is normal and almost never documented. The cost of turning
around is the part people need in order to plan.
**Core:** the original decision, the trigger to reverse it, and the price.

### 👤 One customer's actual path, anonymised
Not a testimonial — a path. Where they came from, what confused them, what they
almost did instead, what finally worked.
**Core:** one real person's sequence, with identifying details removed.

### 💸 What you spent to learn one thing
A course, a hire, a tool, an experiment. Frame it as tuition rather than a
purchase and say whether the lesson was worth the price.
**Core:** the amount, and the one sentence you actually walked away with.

## List

### 🗂️ The five things you check first
When something goes wrong in your area, you have an order you work through, even
if you have never written it down. That order is a list worth posting.
**Core:** your real sequence, in your real order.

### 🔁 What you would do differently the second time
Take one finished project and list the changes you would make. Concrete, ordered,
no abstractions.
**Core:** the project, and at least three specific changes.

### 🧱 The stack, and why each piece survived
Not a tool list — a survival list. Each entry gets the reason it beat the thing
it replaced, and anything you are still unhappy with stays on the list.
**Core:** what you actually use, with the reason attached to each.

### ❌ The recommended things you tried and dropped
Everyone's field has a canon of tools and practices. Some of them did not fit
you. Saying which, and why, is more distinctive than any recommendation.
**Core:** what you dropped, and the specific friction that made you drop it.

### 📚 The three sources you actually go back to
Not a reading list — a re-reading list. Three is the right number; the constraint
is what makes it credible.
**Core:** the three, and the specific reason each earns a second read.

## How-to

### 🪜 The three steps that got you unstuck
Take a problem you were genuinely stuck on and reduce the escape to three steps.
The compression is the work.
**Core:** the stuck state, and the three steps in order.

### 🧩 How to make the boring part faster
Every job has a repetitive middle. If you have found a way through yours in half
the time, that is a post that gets saved.
**Core:** the task, the old way, the new way, and the time saved.

### 🛠️ Rebuild your own workflow in public
Walk through how you would set up your process from scratch today, knowing what
you know. Different from describing what you have, which grew by accident.
**Core:** the process, and at least one thing you would not rebuild.

### 🗺️ The first hour with a new tool
Not a review — the first hour. What you clicked, where you got lost, what you
had to search for. This is the guide the documentation never writes.
**Core:** the tool, and the point where you actually got stuck.

### 🧯 How to recover when it breaks in public
The outage, the wrong send, the typo in the announcement. What you did in the
first ten minutes, and what you would do faster next time.
**Core:** the incident, and your actual sequence of recovery.

## Opinion

### 🔪 The take you keep to yourself
The opinion you hold in your field that you soften in meetings. The nervousness
is the signal; unsoftened is the point.
**Core:** the take, stated without hedging, and the experience behind it.

### 🔄 The thing you changed your mind about
A position you argued for and no longer hold. Say what changed it — evidence,
a project, a person — because that is the part people can use.
**Core:** the old position, the new one, and what moved you.

### 📣 The advice that only works at a size you are not
Guidance from people with ten times your budget or audience, repeated as
universal. Naming the mismatch is a service to everyone smaller.
**Core:** the advice, the scale it assumes, and what breaks at yours.

### 🏷️ A word in your field that has stopped meaning anything
Every industry has a term that has been stretched until it is useless. Say what
it was supposed to mean and what you use instead.
**Core:** the word, and your replacement for it.

### ⚖️ The trade-off nobody names out loud
Every popular approach costs something its advocates skip over. Name the cost
plainly, without arguing that the approach is wrong.
**Core:** the approach, and the specific price you paid for it.

## Question

### ❓ The thing you are genuinely stuck on
Not a rhetorical question with an answer hidden in it — something you actually
do not know. The replies are the point, and people can tell the difference.
**Core:** the real open problem, with enough context to answer usefully.

### 🗳️ A fork you are standing at right now
Two real options, both defensible, decision not yet made. Lay out what you see
on each side and ask what you are missing.
**Core:** the two options and what genuinely worries you about each.

### 👂 The question you get asked that you want to ask back
Something people bring to you constantly, that you suspect they have better
answers to than you do. Turn it around.
**Core:** the question, and why your own answer stopped satisfying you.

### 🔮 What you think changes in your field within a year
A specific prediction with a date, not a trend piece. Being wrong in public
later is part of the value.
**Core:** the prediction, the timeframe, and what would prove you wrong.

### 🧵 Finish the thread
Go back to something you posted weeks ago that promised more and never got it:
the launch with no follow-up, the experiment with no result, the part two.
The audience is already primed and the material already exists.
Only offer this when the earlier post contains a forward promise in its own
words, and quote that promise when you offer it. A story that merely looks
unfinished from outside is not a thread, and the post may not have been public
at all.
**Core:** which earlier post, whether it was really an announcement, and how it
actually turned out.
